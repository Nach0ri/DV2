Wildlife Rescue & Rehabilitation in NSW

Where native animals are rescued, why, and what follows.

By Nachanok Kladsamniang · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2, Monash University · Updated 2 November 2026

In 2024–25 alone, more than 119,000 wildlife rescues were recorded across NSW council areas. This story follows those records from where rescues happen, to why animals need help, to how each rescue ends. Every figure is a recorded rescue, so the charts show where animals were found and reported, not the size of wildlife populations.

Where and when are animals rescued?

Council areas, main causes and the busiest months

01Where are rescues recorded?

Recorded rescues by council area

Rescues are concentrated along the coast and around Sydney. Use the year selector to compare any year from 2013–14 to 2024–25.

A high count shows where animals were found and reported, not necessarily where the risk to wildlife is greatest.

02Where are rescues rising or falling?

Circle size shows volume; colour shows change

Recorded rescues increased in most council areas between 2018–19 and 2024–25. Larger circles mean more rescues in 2024–25; raspberry circles mark councils where rescues increased.

Click a category in the legend to show only councils that rose, stayed the same or fell; double-click to reset.

03What brings animals into care?

Knowing where rescues happen leads to the next question: why. Vehicle collisions are the leading recorded cause, far ahead of any other reason.

Rescues with no reported reason are not ranked.

04When do rescues peak?

Rescue demand through the year

Rescues are not spread evenly across the year: each animal group peaks in a different season.

Hover over a point to see the month, group and count.

Averaged over all years, Birds peak in November (14% of their annual rescues), Mammals in August (10%) and Reptiles in January (13%).

  • Birds have the highest monthly peak: an average of 5,671 rescues in November, 2.1 times the mammal peak (2,694 in August).

Share of the year in spring (Sep–Nov)

25% = the share expected if rescues were spread evenly across the year

Why do animals need rescuing?

Seasonal timing, survival and changing rank of each threat

05Which threats peak in which months?

Each threat has its own seasonal pattern. Rescues of abandoned or orphaned young peak in spring, while heat and drought rescues are concentrated in summer.

Read seasonal patterns with care, as reporting may be incomplete.

06Which reasons have different outcomes?

Rescue volume and outcome by reason

The cause of a rescue is closely linked to whether the animal survives. Each bubble is one reason within one animal group: further right means more rescues, higher means a larger share died, and bubble area shows estimated deaths.

“Survived” is not the same as “released”.

07How are the rankings of rescue reasons changing?

Rising and falling causes

Rankings show whether a cause is becoming more or less common relative to the others. Follow each line from 2013–14 to 2018–19.

09How big is each council if size means rescues?

A cartogram: one circle per council

Redrawing the map so that each council’s size reflects its rescues rather than its land area shows how concentrated rescues are in small coastal councils.

Select a year to see how the pattern changes.

10Which animals are rescued most often?

From places to animals: a small number of sub-groups, led by parrots and possums, account for most rescues. Larger rectangles mean more rescues, 2013–14 to 2018–19.

11What happens after rescue?

From rescue reason to outcome

Following each reason through to its outcome shows which causes leave animals with the poorest chance of survival. Select an animal group to compare.

Flows are estimated from rounded published percentages, 2013–14 to 2018–19.

13Which animals are rescued least often?

At the other end of the scale are sub-groups with very few recorded rescues, 2013–14 to 2018–19.

14How do rescues end for each animal group?
