Wildlife Rescue & Rehabilitation in NSW

Understanding where wildlife is rescued, why animals need help, and what happens next.

Wildlife rescue and rehabilitation services play an important role in protecting native animals across New South Wales. By exploring recorded rescue locations, reported reasons, seasonal patterns, species differences and rehabilitation outcomes, this visualisation investigates what the available data can tell us about wildlife rescue in NSW, and where important gaps remain.

1

Where are rescues happening?

Understanding where rescues happen, what brings animals into care, and when demand rises

01Where are rescues happening?

Mapping recorded rescues across NSW

Explore how recorded wildlife rescues are distributed across NSW. Geographic differences may reflect animal populations, reporting practices and access to rescue services, rather than differences in wildlife risk alone.

Counts depend on where animals are found and reported, so a busy area is not necessarily a dangerous one.

02What brings animals into care?

Compare the reported reasons animals enter rescue and rehabilitation services, highlighting the categories that account for the largest number of recorded cases.

Where a record has no reported reason, it cannot be ranked. Note how many cases fall outside the categories shown.

03When do rescues peak?

Following rescue demand through the year

Compare rescue trends across selected animal groups to investigate when recorded rescue activity rises or falls throughout the year.

2

Why do animals need rescuing?

Understanding reported threats and reasons, and how they change across the year

04Which threats peak when?

Compare monthly rescue patterns across reported reasons to identify periods when particular categories become more common.

Interpret apparent seasonal patterns carefully, especially where reporting is incomplete.

05Which reasons have different outcomes?

Volume and outcome, reason by reason

Examine how case volume and documented outcomes vary between rescue reasons. Differences may be associated with case characteristics and reporting practices, and should not be interpreted as proof of causation.

Use outcome labels exactly as the source defines them. A “positive” outcome is not necessarily a release.

06How are rescue reason rankings changing?

Which reasons are rising or falling?

Follow the ranking of major rescue reasons across reporting years to identify categories that become more or less prominent in the recorded data.

When do rescues happen, and how do outcomes differ?

Comparing regions and species, and following cases after rescue

07Which regions record the most rescues?

Regional change between two periods

Compare regional rescue figures across selected years to identify where recorded rescue activity has increased or decreased.

Rate comparisons require an appropriate and consistent population denominator.

08Which species are rescued most often?

Compare the relative contribution of recorded species to the overall rescue total. Larger rectangles represent species with more recorded rescues, making the distribution of rescue activity easier to explore.

09What happens after rescue?

Sankey diagram

How do outcomes differ across animal groups and rescue reasons?

Compare documented survival and death percentages across rescue reasons for birds, mammals, and reptiles over the study period.

Data are aggregated across the study period (2013–14 to 2018–19) and do not show year-by-year changes. Flow widths are estimated from the published survival and death percentages and rescue totals, because the percentages are rounded.

4

What does the data reveal about species and rehabilitation outcomes?

Exploring trends by species group, less common species and documented outcomes

10Do rescue patterns differ by species group?

Display separate, consistently formatted trend charts for selected species groups to make similarities and differences in recorded rescue patterns easier to identify.

11Which species have the lowest rescue counts?

Rank selected species by recorded rescue counts to highlight less frequently recorded groups.

Low counts do not necessarily mean low conservation risk. Rare records may reflect reporting or data-availability limitations.

12What proportion of rescued animals reach each outcome?

Summarise the proportion of recorded cases associated with each documented outcome.

Make the denominator explicit, and separate released animals from transferred, ongoing, deceased, euthanised or unknown outcomes where these categories are available.
